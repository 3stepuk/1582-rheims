A GUIDED STUDY IN SIX SITTINGS

Strong language

A Catholic guide to speech, charity and truth — swearing, cursing, gossip, slander, mockery and lying.

How to use this

Six short sittings. Read one, then sit with the questions for a few minutes before moving on. You do not need a group; this is written for one person at a desk.

It promises three things: you will know the difference between a coarse word and a grave sin of the tongue; you will not be crushed into scruples; and you will not be let off. If it does those three, it has done its work.

ONE

Why the mouth matters

James does not waste words on this. The tongue is small and sets a forest alight (James 3:5). He is not being poetic; he is being practical. What comes out of the mouth comes from the heart (Matthew 12:34), which is why speech is such a reliable report of what is going on in us.

Think of the last time you heard someone's voice change. Not the words — the voice. The volume going up, the sentences getting shorter, the laugh turning into something with an edge. Something had shifted underneath, and the speech was reporting it.

That is why the Church cares. Not because it is prim about vocabulary, but because speech is where charity becomes audible. You can hold a grudge silently for years; the moment it reaches your mouth and another person's ear, it has done something in the world.

And James gives the scrupulous reader a verse to hold: “we all make many mistakes” (3:2). The point of this study is not to make you afraid of your own mouth.

REFLECT

  • When did my speech last change without my deciding to change it?
  • What was the room like — and who was in it?
  • What was I protecting?

TWO

What “swearing” actually means

The English word covers four different things, and mixing them is the first confusion to clear.

  • Profanity — crude vocabulary. If it uses God's name carelessly, that belongs to the Second Commandment (CCC 2149), not the Eighth.
  • Cursing — invoking harm on someone (Aquinas, ST II-II q.76). Not the same as saying “damn” at a stubbed toe.
  • Oaths — calling God to witness. Lawful with grave and right reason (CCC 2154); perjury is grave (CCC 2163).
  • Blasphemy — contemptuous speech against God, “in itself a grave sin” (CCC 2148).

Then, within the eighth commandment, these are different acts with different objects:

  • Reviling (contumelia) — an insult said to the person's face.
  • Detraction — a true fault told to people who had no business hearing it, without objectively valid reason.
  • Calumny — the same damage done with something false.
  • Tale-bearing — speech whose end is to divide friends.
  • Derision — mockery that holds a person up to shame.
  • Lying — saying what is false, with intent to deceive.
  • Boasting — a lie about oneself; and behind it, vainglory, the appetite for glory.
  • Obscenity — deliberately filthy speech to amuse or arouse, which Aquinas treats as mortal in itself (ST q.168 a.3).

The distinction that catches people: detraction tells the truth and is still wrong, because the fault was not yours to publish. That one feels like honesty, and is not.

THE SCHEME THAT RESOLVES MOST OF IT

Speech attacks four different goods, and the sin is named by which:

  • Truth — lying, boasting.
  • Justice — detraction, calumny, reviling.
  • Religion — blasphemy, perjury.
  • Temperance and modesty — obscenity.

A word can be rude and attack none of them. Gravity follows the good attacked, not the loudness of the word.

REFLECT

  • Which of those eight acts have I actually committed this week — not which have I felt like?
  • Have I ever defended gossip as “only the truth”?
  • Have I used a coarse word that attacked nobody? Say so plainly; that is not a sin of the tongue.

THREE

What the Church binds, and what is advice

The Eighth Commandment grounds the conscience in truth and covenant fidelity (CCC 2464); its justice is spelled out at CCC 2469 and 2479. What it binds is specific:

  • Rash judgement — assuming someone's fault, even tacitly, without sufficient foundation (CCC 2477). The remedy is 2478: interpret favourably, then ask.
  • Detraction — disclosing another's faults without objectively valid reason (CCC 2477; ST q.73).
  • Calumny — false accusation (CCC 2477; ST q.68 a.3).
  • Reviling — insult to the face (ST q.72).
  • Cursing — invoking harm (ST q.76; near CCC 2302–2303).
  • Lying — never licit (CCC 2485), venial by default, mortal through grave harm (CCC 2484).
  • Blasphemy — grave in itself (CCC 2148).

And what is advice rather than obligation: tone, register, the volume of an argument, the habit of talking bigger than you feel. These matter — they shape the room and they shape you — but none of them is grave matter by itself.

So say it plainly: the scrupulous reader is not required to treat every coarse word as a sin; the careless reader is not permitted to treat contempt as nothing.

Scandal is a qualifier, not a fifth category: speech that leads another into sin (CCC 2284–2287) is graver where the speaker has authority or the hearer is weak. Strong language in front of a child is not the same act as strong language in a car.

REFLECT

  • Where have I treated advice as law — and frightened myself with it?
  • Where have I treated a grave matter as advice — and let myself off?
  • Who is weaker in my hearing, and what have they heard from me?

REFLECT

  • Take one thing you said this week. Which row does it belong in — if any?
  • Was it said to the person, or behind their back? The answer changes the sin.

FIVE

The Bible's own tongue

If you expect Scripture to be prim, it will surprise you. The Bible is not squeamish; it is careful. It knows the difference between strong speech and wicked speech, and it uses the first.

Paul counts everything he once valued as skýbala (Philippians 3:8) — a word closer to “crap” or “dung” than to “rubbish”.

Paul again, on the circumcisers troubling the Galatians: he wishes they would go the whole way and castrate themselves (Galatians 5:12). That is a joke, and a savage one, in Scripture.

Jesus calls men “whitewashed tombs” and a “brood of vipers”. He uses raca — a slur — in Matthew 5:22, and then tells you what to do about it.

The prophets are blunt about adultery and idolatry in ways we would blush at in church.

So what is the difference? Not the strength of the word but its target and its truth. Scripture's strong speech is aimed at sin, hypocrisy and false teaching; it is not aimed at a person's honour for the pleasure of the room. When it is, the same book condemns it: blessing God and cursing men made in his likeness out of the same mouth (James 3:9–10).

A caution for the scrupulous: “every careless word” (Matthew 12:36) is a warning about accountability, not a grading system. The context is the accusation against Jesus, and gravity is still measured by the Catechism's conditions — grave matter, full knowledge, deliberate consent.

REFLECT

  • When I am strong-tongued, is it aimed at sin or at a person?
  • What would it look like to be as blunt as Paul and as careful as James?

SIX

Repair, and a rule of life

Three rules, and then the examination.

  1. Presence. Never speak of an absent person in a way you would not speak in front of her. This one rule removes most detraction before it starts.
  2. Pause. Before speaking about someone in frustration, stop. Three seconds is enough to ask: what is this serving — truth and charity, or my image?
  3. Real reparation. Reviling needs an apology, to the person. Detraction and calumny need the good name restored — correcting the false impression, going back to the people you told. Reparation is measured by the damage (CCC 2487), and an apology that leaves the false impression standing is not reparation.

EXAMINATION — FOUR QUESTIONS

  1. Have I used strong language to project an image, mask insecurity, or win a room?
  2. Have I repeated a true fault about someone who had no business hearing it?
  3. Have I said something false about a person and left it standing?
  4. Where I have harmed a name, what have I actually done to restore it?

PRAYER

“Set a guard over my mouth, O Lord; keep watch over the door of my lips.” — Psalm 141:3 (140:3 in the Vulgate)

Lord, make my speech true and kind at once. Where I have used words to make myself larger, humble me. Where I have harmed someone's name, give me the courage to put it right. Let my speech be seasoned with salt, and give grace to those who hear it. Amen.

REFLECT

  • Which of the three rules will I keep this week — one, not all three?
  • Is there one name I need to restore, and one person to whom I need to go?
